Surplus or Bust

A Risk Management Simulator

A 30-minute game on the core of asset liability management, built on real insurers' annual reports

Run a real US life insurer through five quarters of stress: ask the AI team, sign the trades, then explain the result to Vera.

One-line pitch. Thirty minutes is the design target; the pilot will measure it. The cast: Vera the CRO, who also marks your explanations; Morty the over-cautious actuary; Bonnie the yield-hungry portfolio manager; Swap Dogg the trader who hedges everything; Seamore, who reads the 10-K filings to calibrate each company.
The problem

New analysts must learn why a 1-point rate fall costs an insurer a fifth of its surplus

SQL in 3 weeks→3 days→3 hours→ALM in 30 minutes?

Why: promises are paid decades out, so lower rates raise what they are worth today. The bonds rise too, but less, because they are shorter.

Jackson Financial, rates 1 point lower, in the game's engine.

The SQL story: a YouTuber's "SQL in three weeks", a colleague's "three days", a manager's joke about "three hours". My company hires risk analysts from strong liberal arts colleges with no finance background; turnover is high and ramp-up takes months [MICAH: optional internal figure]. Today they learn by shadowing and mistakes. SQL is a tool; ALM is a way of thinking. The chart is why it is hard: surplus is the small gap between two big moving numbers, and a one-point rate fall takes 22% of Jackson's.

How the agents work

Vera sends each question to the one or two right specialists; the engine owns every number

Why it matters: the AI can be wrong in words, never in numbers. It cannot set a score or trade without your signature.

Six agents, five characters (Vera also marks explanations), one model (gpt-6-luna). Specialists read only their side of the balance sheet through read-only tools. Swap Dogg can draft a ticket; only the learner signs. A separate examiner marks explanations; Seamore reads 10-Ks with code-checked quotes. Team 19/19 facts vs Solo 16/19, at 3.5x the cost: kept for teaching, so learners weigh biased experts.

Demo · 2

Take the shock, then explain it to Vera

Four checks against the engine's answer key. Miss one, and Vera says which and gives a hint; you try again.

After the trades, The Market Daily reports the quarter with a real-life echo (2022). Then the learner explains it. Vera's four true/false checks (main driver, why it moved surplus, second factor, what your trades did) are summed by code, never taken from model text. The correction loop is what teaches: in the demo game, three of five quarters needed a second try.
