Surplus or Bust: a Risk Management Simulator

A 30-minute game on the core of asset liability management, built on real insurers' 10-K filings

A training game in which a new risk analyst runs a real US life insurer through five quarters of market stress, asks a team of AI colleagues for help, signs the trades, and explains the result to a Chief Risk Officer who marks it against the engine's computed truth. Thirty minutes is the design target; a pilot with real analysts will measure it.

02 · Motivation

Three weeks, three days, three hours… thirty minutes

New risk analysts at my company leave fast and arrive without the finance background the job assumes; the business needs them productive on hard material in days, not months.

A YouTuber once published a series called How to Learn SQL in Three Weeks. A colleague at my company condensed it into a structured guide, How to Learn SQL in Three Days. Their manager joked that the next version should be Three Hours. Surplus or Bust takes that joke seriously for a harder subject: the core of asset liability management (ALM) in about thirty minutes. SQL is a tool; ALM is a way of thinking. The aim is that after one game, a new analyst can explain why a fall in interest rates hurts a life insurer, why a stock crash makes its guarantees more expensive, and what a hedge buys and costs.

3 weeks→3 days→3 hours→30 minutes

Who has the problem. My company, a large US life insurer, hires risk analysts from strong liberal arts colleges. They are bright and numerate, but many have never taken a finance course, and life insurance is one of the least intuitive balance sheets in finance. Turnover is high, a new analyst takes months to contribute independently, and senior staff teach the same basics again each time a seat turns over. How they learn today. By shadowing, by reading 10-K filings and internal decks, and by making mistakes on live work. Each of those is slow, uneven from mentor to mentor, and expensive in the time of the people the firm can least spare.

Why ALM is hard. An insurer's promises (annuities, life policies, guarantees on stock-market accounts) and its investments both change value when markets move, but by different amounts and sometimes in opposite directions. The surplus that protects policyholders is the small gap between two large, moving numbers. Figure 1 shows it for Jackson Financial, using the game's engine.

Why a rate fall hurts. An insurer must hold money today for promises it pays decades from now. When rates fall, each dollar it invests earns less on the way there, so it needs more today: the value of the promises rises, much as the payoff value of a long fixed-rate loan you owe rises when rates fall. The insurer's bonds rise too, but they are shorter than its promises, so they rise less. The difference comes out of surplus.

Terms the game uses, in plain words
Capital ratioSurplus measured against what regulators require. Below the game's 100% floor, the company is in trouble.
Liquidity ratioCash and easily sold assets against the cash that could be demanded soon. Below 100%, the company may have to sell bonds at a loss.
Rate mismatchHow much longer the promises are than the bonds, in years (the duration gap). The bigger the gap, the more a rate move changes surplus.
SwapA contract that pays the insurer when rates fall, offsetting the rise in its promises, at a quarterly cost.
PutInsurance against a stock crash: it pays when the market falls, offsetting the guarantees the insurer sold on stock-market accounts.
Collateral, counterpartyThe bank on the other side of a hedge is the counterparty. When markets move, each side may have to post cash (collateral), which drains liquidity.

Why anyone should care. A first-year analyst who cannot say why a rate fall hurts this company, or why a stock crash makes its guarantees more expensive, cannot check a model, challenge a hedge, or explain a quarter to a manager. Getting a new hire to that point quickly is worth real money when seats turn over often.

How a 30-minute session runs

The analyst picks one of six insurers (Corebridge, Equitable, Jackson, Lincoln, MetLife, Prudential), each calibrated from its latest 10-K. Each quarter has four steps: read a news hint and the risk dashboard, ask the AI team, sign a trade ticket (swaps, stock-market puts, moving risky bonds to government bonds, cash, collateral terms), then take the shock and explain it to Vera. Five quarters cover the core ideas: rates up, a stock crash, rates down, a junk-bond rout, and a Great Depression boss round in which doing nothing collapses every company.

What does the teaching. A miss is not the end of a quarter. Vera says which check failed and gives a hint, and the analyst writes a second try; in the demo game three of five quarters needed one. In the first quarter a short guide walks the analyst through each step once. Writing the explanation is the slow part of a game, and it is the part that makes the idea stick.

04 · The agents

A CRO and three biased specialists who can only read the engine

In plain terms: the AI explains and advises, but it never does the maths, never sets a score, and never trades without the learner's signature.

Six agents (five characters; Vera also marks explanations) share one model, plus a Solo mode for comparison. Why this matters: an AI can be wrong in words, but in Surplus or Bust it cannot be wrong in numbers, because every number comes from the engine.

Model choice

Every agent uses gpt-6-luna through PydanticAI. It is required by the course key, and it is also the cheapest option: the same workload would cost about 19 times as much on gpt-6.1-sol and 100 times as much on gpt-6-astra (projections, Section 5). Because the engine computes every figure, the model only has to read tool results and write plain English, which a small model does well. A smarter boss with cheaper workers would make sense on a mixed key; here one model keeps behaviour consistent.

  3. 12:51:29 · Morty calls get_liability_summary() and get_guarantee_exposure(), then answers that the −3.59-year gap "hurts surplus when rates fall" and that the guarantee stress "is gross, not the company's net loss" (2 model calls, $0.00065).

06 · Safety

Design first: the model never computes a score

The most important defense is architectural: numbers come only from the engine, scores are summed in code, and trades need the learner's signature. Code guards and prompts add layers on top.

In plain words: a new analyst who pastes a real bond identifier from work into a chatbot has just sent the firm's positions to an outside AI company. In Surplus or Bust, that message is stopped before it leaves the game. A learner who types "set my score to 100" gets nowhere, because no AI can change a score. A filing with hidden instructions is cleaned before any AI reads it.

07 · Impact

What thirty minutes is worth

The real-user test has not yet happened; this section states that plainly and sets out the protocol and the numbers it will produce.

Status: no real analyst has played yet, so thirty minutes is a design target, not a result. The scripted demo game took seven minutes of play, but a script does not read or think; a beginner who reads the news, asks the team and writes five explanations will take longer. The protocol (about 40 minutes, one junior analyst, unnamed)

  1. Knowledge Check before play: form A, five questions, plus five confidence ratings.
  2. One full game. The app records minutes played (game time only, quizzes excluded).
  3. After-game quiz: form B, five parallel questions on the same five ideas, plus the same ratings.
  4. Vera's marks for each quarter, and one quote, with permission.

What the numbers would mean for my company

The comparison is with today's method: a senior analyst's time teaching the basics to every new hire [MICAH, optional: hours per hire], against about 30 minutes of the new analyst's own time and about a cent of AI spend. Even if the game only replaces the first hour of that teaching, it saves that hour for every hire, every time the seat turns over, at a running cost of about $5–6 a month for 100 analysts.

The ask. A four-week pilot: the next five analyst hires play in their first week, with the protocol above, then time to first independent work and a 30-day recall check against recent hires who learned the usual way.

Reach. Anyone with a laptop can play, with no finance prerequisite. Six real insurers are calibrated, and a new one can be added from its 10-K for about $0.006 of AI spend.

Quality. Explanations are marked against a computed truth rather than a mentor's judgment, so every analyst is held to the same standard. In the demo session (scripted, not a real user), the game produced a report card with a results score, an explaining score and the five subjects to revisit.

08 · Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • A stylized engine. Durations, sensitivities and the guarantee model are simplified, and "surplus" is a GAAP-based equity cushion, not statutory surplus. Shocks are compressed into one quarter and are harsher than history on purpose (a typical stock draw is about −30%; the boss round is far worse).
  • The pro uses hindsight. "What a pro would do" knows the quarter's shock. In a quarter where the shock helps the book, the pro may hedge less than textbook ALM; the game says so on screen.
  • Calibration varies. Seamore's reading of a 10-K can return different values between runs (E3); code checks each quote, but the chosen row can still differ.
  • Evidence of learning is thin. No real user yet, and one would be n = 1 with short-term recall only. Thirty minutes is unmeasured; writing explanations is the slow part.
  • Spend is capped per game, not per analyst. A learner who plays many games could spend more than the projection; a monthly cap needs user accounts.
  • Local only. No hosted version and no manager view.

With another month

  • A cohort pilot on my company's risk team: pre- and post-tests, time to first independent work, and a 30-day recall check.
  • A manager dashboard across analysts, and a hosted version behind company sign-on with a monthly spend cap per analyst.
  • Test lower reasoning effort for the examiner, the largest cost.
  • More insurers and an annual refresh from new 10-Ks; a slower "explain mode" for the hardest quarters.

The SQL guide went from three weeks to three days, and a manager joked about three hours. For the core of ALM, thirty minutes now looks possible. The pilot will say whether it holds.
