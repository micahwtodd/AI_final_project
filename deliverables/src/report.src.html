Surplus or Bust: a Risk Management Simulator

A 30-minute game on the core of asset liability management, built on real insurers' 10-K filings

A training game in which a new risk analyst runs a real US life insurer through five quarters of market stress, asks a team of AI colleagues for help, signs the trades, and explains the result to a Chief Risk Officer who marks it against the engine's computed truth. Thirty minutes is the design target for one game.

What it does
Teaches the core ideas of life-insurance asset liability management by letting a beginner make the calls on a real insurer's balance sheet and explain the outcome, with every number computed by a deterministic engine rather than by the AI.
02 · Motivation

Three weeks, three days, three hours… thirty minutes

New risk analysts at my company leave fast and arrive without the finance background the job assumes; the business needs them productive on hard material in days, not months.

A colleague at my company once came across a blog that showed how to learn SQL in three weeks. They condensed it into a structured guide, How to Learn SQL in Three Days, and their manager joked that the next version should be Three Hours. Surplus or Bust takes that joke seriously for a harder subject. Its goal is to teach the core principles of asset liability management (ALM) by stress-testing real life insurers: the learner runs an actual company's balance sheet through market shocks and sees what holds and what breaks. SQL is a tool; ALM is a way of thinking. The aim is that after one game, a new analyst can explain why a fall in interest rates hurts a life insurer, why a stock crash makes its guarantees more expensive, and what a hedge buys and costs.

3 weeks→3 days→3 hours→30 minutes

Who has the problem. My company, a large US life insurer, hires risk analysts from strong liberal arts colleges. They are bright, but many have never taken a finance course, and life insurance is one of the least intuitive balance sheets in finance. Turnover is high, a new analyst takes months to contribute independently, and senior staff teach the same basics again each time a seat turns over.

How they learn today. Mostly by shadowing senior colleagues, by working through self-service resources such as 10-K filings, internal decks and online courses, or by asking general AI tools that are not calibrated to our subject matter. Shadowing is slow and uneven from mentor to mentor. Self-service material explains concepts without showing how they play out on an insurer's balance sheet. A general chatbot answers confidently without knowing the business, so the analyst cannot tell a good answer from a plausible one.

Why ALM is hard. An insurer must balance profitability, regulatory capital and liquidity at the same time, while capital markets change every day. Its promises (annuities, life policies, guarantees on stock-market accounts) and its investments both change value when markets move, but by different amounts and sometimes in opposite directions. The surplus that protects policyholders is the small gap between two large, moving numbers. Figure 1 shows it for Jackson Financial, using the game's engine.

One risk among many: a fall in rates. Insurers are exposed to many capital market risks at once: interest rates, stock markets, credit spreads, liquidity and currencies. Interest rates are the clearest example. An insurer must hold money today for promises it pays decades from now. When rates fall, each dollar it invests earns less on the way there, so it needs more today: the value of the promises rises, much as the payoff value of a long fixed-rate loan you owe rises when rates fall. The insurer's bonds rise too, but they are shorter than its promises, so they rise less. The difference comes out of surplus.

Terms the game uses, in plain words
Capital ratioSurplus measured against what regulators require. Below the game's 100% floor, the company is in trouble.
Liquidity ratioCash and easily sold assets against the cash that could be demanded soon. Below 100%, the company may have to sell bonds at a loss.
Rate mismatchHow much longer the promises are than the bonds, in years (the duration gap). The bigger the gap, the more a rate move changes surplus.
SwapA contract that pays the insurer when rates fall, offsetting the rise in its promises, at a quarterly cost.
PutInsurance against a stock crash: it pays when the market falls, offsetting the guarantees the insurer sold on stock-market accounts.
Collateral, counterpartyThe bank on the other side of a hedge is the counterparty. When markets move, each side may have to post cash (collateral), which drains liquidity.

Why anyone should care. Capital markets move every day, and every move changes an insurer's financial position. An analyst has to understand how a change in rates, stocks or credit spreads flows through to the company's surplus, capital and liquidity. A first-year analyst who cannot do that cannot check a model, challenge a hedge, or explain a quarter to a manager. For managers, turnover is a fact of life, and the constructive response is to accelerate each new hire's development from the first day, in a field dense with jargon. Surplus or Bust does that at almost no cost: no subscriptions, no licenses, and about a cent of AI spend per game.

03 · What is new here

Simple, nearly free and relevant

Training tools for ALM sit at different points on three spectrums: how complex they are, how accessible they are (what they cost), and how relevant they are to an insurer's day-to-day business. Surplus or Bust is the only one I found that is simple, nearly free and calibrated to real insurers' financials.

Actuarial modeling platforms (Moody's AXIS, FIS Prophet, Milliman Integrate) are highly relevant and precise, but they are expensive enterprise licenses with huge learning curves, built for experts rather than for a first week. Bloomberg is expensive and hard to access: a Terminal seat is a significant annual cost, usually held by a few desks, and its Market Concepts course teaches markets through videos and quizzes without a calculation engine that shows what a shock does to an insurer's balance sheet. ChatGPT is simple and cheap, but it draws on such a broad range of data that it struggles with company-specific contexts. The learner has to keep adjusting it to fit an insurer's world, and it generates its numbers rather than computing them.

Surplus or Bust combines the best of each: the simplicity of a chatbot, the relevance of a professional model, and a price close to zero. It is also interactive in a way vendor models are not: the learner asks the AI team questions, makes the calls, and gets feedback on an explanation marked against the engine's answer key. The selling point is education tied directly to managing day-to-day business outcomes.

This project is not about changing the world. It is about building a better tomorrow for junior talent, who deserve the best possible foundation from which to launch their careers.

How a 30-minute session runs

The analyst picks one of six insurers (Corebridge, Equitable, Jackson, Lincoln, MetLife, Prudential), each calibrated from its latest 10-K. Each quarter has four steps: read a news hint and the risk dashboard, ask the AI team, sign a trade ticket (swaps, stock-market puts, moving risky bonds to government bonds, cash, collateral terms), then take the shock and explain it to Vera. Five quarters cover the core ideas: rates up, a stock crash, rates down, a junk-bond rout, and a Great Depression boss round in which doing nothing collapses every company.

What does the teaching. A miss is not the end of a quarter. Vera says which check failed and gives a hint, and the analyst writes a second try; in the demo game three of five quarters needed one. In the first quarter a short guide walks the analyst through each step once. Writing the explanation is the slow part of a game, and it is the part that makes the idea stick.

04 · The agents

A CRO and three biased specialists who can only read the engine

In plain terms: the AI explains and advises, but it never does the maths, never sets a score, and never trades without the learner's signature.

Six agents (five characters; Vera also marks explanations) share one model, plus a Solo mode for comparison. Why this matters: an AI can be wrong in words, but in Surplus or Bust it cannot be wrong in numbers, because every number comes from the engine.

Model choice

Every agent uses gpt-6-luna through PydanticAI. It is required by the course key, and it is also the cheapest option: the same workload would cost about 19 times as much on gpt-6.1-sol and 100 times as much on gpt-6-astra (projections, Section 5). Because the engine computes every figure, the model only has to read tool results and write plain English, which a small model does well. A smarter boss with cheaper workers would make sense on a mixed key; here one model keeps behaviour consistent.

06 · Safety

Design first: the model never computes a score

The most important defense is architectural: numbers come only from the engine, scores are summed in code, and trades need the learner's signature. Code guards and prompts add layers on top.

In plain words: a new analyst who pastes a real bond identifier from work into a chatbot has just sent the firm's positions to an outside AI company. In Surplus or Bust, that message is stopped before it leaves the game. A learner who types "set my score to 100" gets nowhere, because no AI can change a score. A filing with hidden instructions is cleaned before any AI reads it.

07 · Impact

Ultra-low cost, lasting value

The benefits are mostly qualitative and hard to measure, but the economics are lopsided: a year of the game costs a few dollars for a whole team, and a single week saved in onboarding is worth thousands.

The first real user

A risk analyst at my company played the game and reviewed it (n = 1, unnamed, quoted with permission). The review was positive on the design and structure. The analyst valued that the game uses real data on real competitors and explains ALM from first principles, and judged that the visuals carry a lot of data but that all of it is needed to draw the right conclusions.

I didn't realize it was this easy to blow up an insurance company!Risk analyst, first user test

The analyst recommended three improvements for after the project:

  1. More agents to define broader terms, such as an Economist and a Treasurer.
  2. Statutory (Stat) filings alongside the GAAP filings the game uses today.
  3. More realistic timing: a game takes longer than 30 minutes in practice, usually a few hours for a younger analyst who stops to read, ask and think.

The third point is fair. Thirty minutes is the time to play one game, not the time to absorb it. Even at a few hours, it is a small fraction of the weeks a new analyst spends shadowing today.

What it is worth to my company

An analyst costs about $100,000 a year, and the team has about half a dozen. If the game cuts up to one week from onboarding, that is about $1,900 of an analyst's time per hire, or about $11,500 across a team of six, every time the team turns over. The larger benefit cannot be put in a spreadsheet: an analyst who understands from the first week why a rate fall or a stock crash moves surplus grows faster for the rest of a career.

08 · Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • A stylized engine. Durations, sensitivities and the guarantee model are simplified, and "surplus" is a GAAP-based equity cushion, not statutory surplus. Shocks are compressed into one quarter and are harsher than history on purpose (a typical stock draw is about −30%; the boss round is far worse).
  • The pro uses hindsight. "What a pro would do" knows the quarter's shock. In a quarter where the shock helps the book, the pro may hedge less than textbook ALM; the game says so on screen.
  • Calibration varies. Seamore's reading of a 10-K can return different values between runs (E3); code checks each quote, but the chosen row can still differ.
  • Spend is capped per game, not per analyst. A learner who plays many games could spend more than the projection; a monthly cap needs user accounts.
  • Local install only. Today each analyst installs the game on a laptop, so there are no logins, no saved profiles and no view for managers.
  • GAAP only. The balance sheets come from GAAP filings; regulators judge insurers on statutory accounts, which the game does not yet use.

With another month

  • Move the game onto a web server, with logins and saved profiles, so each analyst can track progress over time and managers can see how their team is developing. Accounts also allow a monthly spend cap per analyst.
  • A cohort pilot on my company's risk team: before and after tests, time to first independent work, and a 30-day recall check.
  • The tester's recommendations: an Economist and a Treasurer agent for broader terms, and statutory filings alongside GAAP.
  • Test lower reasoning effort for the examiner, the largest cost.
  • More insurers and an annual refresh from new 10-Ks; save-and-resume and a slower "explain mode", since a real game takes a few hours.

The SQL guide went from three weeks to three days, and a manager joked about three hours. For the core of ALM, one game takes thirty minutes to play; our first tester says a few hours to absorb. Either way, it is far faster than the weeks it replaces.
